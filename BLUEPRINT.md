# GharSe — Neighbourhood Home Economy Marketplace

> Turn every home into an opportunity, and every local skill into an income.

A hyperlocal marketplace connecting **verified women home entrepreneurs** with customers who want
affordable home-made food, handmade products and trusted home-based services.

`GharSe` is a working title. Alternatives worth testing: *Anganam*, *HomePlate*, *Ghar Ka*, *Aangan*.
Check trademark and domain availability before printing anything.

---

## 1. The problem, from both sides

**Demand side.** People in PGs, hostels, shared flats and offices want simple home-cooked food and
can't get it — their home is in another city. Restaurant delivery is the only option and it is
expensive, oily, and repetitive.

**Supply side.** A homemaker often already knows how to cook, stitch, embroider, bake, string
flowers, teach, or craft. What she does not have is **demand, discovery, logistics, payments, trust
and technology**. Those six things are the entire product.

---

## 2. Three verticals, launched in sequence

| Phase | Vertical | What it is |
|-------|----------|------------|
| 1 | **Home Kitchen** | Daily meals, tiffin, biryani, festival and bulk orders — the wedge |
| 2 | **Home Creations** | Garlands, tailoring, saree work, handloom, pickles, crafts, gifts |
| 3 | **Home Services** | Alterations, ironing, mehendi, tuition, event cooking, packing work |

Food is the wedge because it is the only category with **daily frequency**. Frequency builds the
customer habit; the other two categories then ride on an audience that already opens the app.

**Safety principle for phase 3:** work happens at the woman's home. The item is picked up and
dropped back. We do not send a woman alone into a stranger's house in the pilot. This is a product
constraint, not a nice-to-have.

---

## 3. What makes it different from Swiggy / Amazon / Urban Company

Swiggy sells restaurants. Amazon sells products. Urban Company sells labour hours.

This sells **a person's skill, from her home, to her neighbourhood** — with four mechanics none of
them have:

1. **Subscriptions.** A PG resident buys 26 lunches, not one lunch. Predictable income for her,
   predictable spend for him.
2. **Meal preferences carried across the platform.** Diet, spice, oil, allergies, budget — set once,
   sent to every kitchen you subscribe to. (Call it *Meal Preferences*, not "prescription" — the
   moment you say prescription you are making a medical claim you cannot back.)
3. **Reverse marketplace.** The customer posts "50 jasmine garlands by Saturday, ₹2,000" and nearby
   women bid. Standard marketplaces only run in one direction.
4. **Community pickup points.** Fifteen meals arrive at one PG reception at 12:45. This is the only
   version of the delivery math that actually works — see §5.

---

## 4. The pricing promise — and the one correction

The instinct is "home food must always cost less than hotel food." Do not promise that. Some
home-made items — small-batch, better ingredients, hand-work — legitimately cost *more*, and forcing
those women to underprice would squeeze the exact people this platform exists to lift.

The promise is narrower and defensible:

> **Affordable everyday home food.**

The app shows a **HomePrice** comparison per listing — your price against the typical outside price,
entered by the seller — so the saving is visible where it is real, and absent where it isn't.

## 5. Unit economics (illustrative, at the app's default settings)

Defaults shipped in the MVP: **10%** commission on item value — split as **8% platform**
(onboarding, verification, the app) and **2% processing & support** (gateway charges, the team's
coordination) — plus **₹15** home delivery, **₹5** community pickup point, **₹0** self pickup.

Both lines are shown separately to both sides. One blended number invites the question "what am I
paying for?"; two named ones answer it.

One ₹85 meal, delivered to a PG pickup point:

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Customer pays | **₹90** |
| Home entrepreneur receives | **₹76.50** |
| Platform fee (8%) | ₹6.80 |
| Processing & support (2%) | ₹1.70 |
| Delivery | ₹5.00 |

Her day, cooking 20 meals at ₹85, with ingredients and gas at roughly ₹50/meal:

- Revenue to her: 20 × ₹76.50 = **₹1,530**
- Cost of goods: 20 × ₹50 = ₹1,000
- Contribution: **₹530/day** → roughly **₹13,250 over 25 days**, from one meal slot

**Why 10% and not 30%.** Commission has to be judged against her *margin*, not her price. A
restaurant prices at 3–4× food cost and survives a 25% take; a home cook prices at ~1.6×, so a ₹85
meal carries about ₹33 of margin. A 30% commission takes ₹25.50 of it — roughly 77% of her profit,
leaving ₹7.50 a meal. She would quit within weeks, and she would be right to. Ten percent is not
generosity; it is the rate at which supply survives.

Two slots, or higher-margin custom and festival orders, move that materially. These are
illustrative figures for planning, not an earnings promise — publishing guaranteed-income numbers is
how gig platforms get themselves into trouble.

**The honest problem: ₹15 does not pay a rider.** A single 2 km drop costs ₹25–40 all-in. The unit
only closes when one trip carries several orders:

- 1 order per trip → loses money on every order
- 5 orders per trip at ₹15 → ₹75 per trip, roughly break-even
- 12 meals to one PG reception at ₹5 → ₹60 for one stop, comfortably positive

So delivery design is not an ops detail, it is the business model. Dense cells, batched runs,
pickup points. Never one meal, nine kilometres.

## 6. Revenue streams

| Stream | When | Note |
|--------|------|------|
| Commission — 8% platform + 2% processing | Day 1 | Deliberately thin — a ₹100 meal cannot carry a 30% take rate |
| Delivery fee | Day 1 | Should approach cost, not profit, until density exists |
| Customer meal subscriptions | Phase 1 | The compounding one |
| Seller Pro (₹99 / ₹199 / ₹499 per month) | Phase 2 | Photos, priority placement, analytics |
| Sponsored placement | Phase 2 | Only after organic discovery genuinely works |
| **Corporate meal contracts** | Phase 2–3 | A 100-person office, distributed across 10 kitchens |

Corporate is the sleeper. It converts one sales conversation into a hundred daily subscriptions and
turns the network into a distributed kitchen rather than a food-delivery app.

## 7. Trust is the moat

Every seller profile carries: KYC, verified phone, bank account, location, FSSAI number for food,
kitchen check, hygiene training, rating, orders completed, repeat customers, cancellation rate.

Food businesses in India require FSSAI registration or a licence. Most homemakers do not have one
and will not navigate the process alone — **so the platform walks them through the application**.
That help is not overhead, it is the onboarding pitch and a real barrier for anyone copying us.

The MVP enforces this: a food listing cannot go live until the provider is admin-verified **and**
has an FSSAI number on file. Products and services publish immediately.

---

## 8. Fair economics, stated in public

Indian gig-work regulation is moving, and Karnataka is actively building a platform-worker welfare
framework. Rather than defend a take rate later, build the position now:

- Commission capped at 10% and itemised on every single order, to both sides
- The customer sees exactly what reached the cook
- **Price guidance, not haggling.** Daily meal prices are fixed and transparent; the team sets a
  fair range per category in the admin, and the app warns a seller who prices outside it — or below
  her own cost. Bidding stays only where price is genuinely bespoke: custom, bulk and festival
  orders. Haggling over a ₹90 lunch costs both sides more than it moves, and only the cook loses
- The admin dashboard reports "% of GMV that reached the women" as a headline KPI

A platform that empowers customers by squeezing the women it claims to serve has no story worth
telling. The transparency is the story.

## 9. The Bengaluru pilot

**One cell. Not a city.** HSR Layout + the PG clusters around it.

| Target | Number |
|--------|--------|
| Home entrepreneurs onboarded | 25 |
| Verified for food | 15 |
| Customers | 200 |
| Pickup points (PG receptions, office lobbies) | 5 |
| Meal slots | Lunch first, dinner second |

Run it semi-manually. A WhatsApp group and two riders beat a rewritten app in week one.

**The metric that decides everything: meals per customer per month.** Downloads are vanity.
If a PG resident orders 18 lunches in a month, this is a business. If he orders 3, it is a novelty
and no amount of engineering fixes it. The admin dashboard reports the repeat rate directly.

Secondary: kitchen utilisation (are the 20 slots selling out?), on-time %, cancellation rate,
subscription retention past month one.

## 10. Where AI actually earns its place

Not chatbots. Two specific jobs:

1. **Voice-to-listing.** She says, in Kannada, "tomorrow I can make 20 chicken biryanis at ₹140" and
   a complete listing is created — title, price, quantity, slot, diet tags. Typing an e-commerce
   listing form is the single biggest onboarding barrier for this supply base.
2. **Photo-to-listing.** A picture of a saree, garland or craft becomes the product page.

Plus matching: given a customer's preferences and budget, find the compatible kitchens.

Language matters more than features here — Kannada, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Bengali,
Marathi, English. **Voice-first beats menu-first** for this supply side.

## 11. Risks, named honestly

| Risk | Response |
|------|----------|
| Delivery economics at low density | Pickup points and tight cells before any expansion |
| Food safety incident | FSSAI gate, hygiene training, kitchen check, fast delisting, insurance |
| Supply churn | Subscriptions give predictable income — the main retention tool |
| A big player copying it | The moat is verified supply and neighbourhood density, not code |
| Regulatory shift on gig work | Low take rate and transparency built in from day one |
| Payment and refund disputes | Escrow-style hold, refund policy visible before the first order |

## 12. Roadmap

**Now (this repo).** Working MVP: registration, verification gating, listings, orders with a live
fee split, subscriptions, reverse marketplace with bids, ratings, admin dashboard.

**Next.** Real payments (Razorpay/UPI) with payouts to sellers · WhatsApp notifications ·
photo uploads · Postgres instead of SQLite · geolocation and true distance instead of area strings ·
rider app · voice listing.

**Then.** Corporate contracts · seller subscription tiers · second cell only after cell one's repeat
rate holds.

## 13. Running the MVP

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
streamlit run home_economy_app.py
```

First login: `admin` / `admin@123` — change it under **Fees & Settings**. Home entrepreneurs and
customers self-register. The app starts empty by design; it fills with real activity.
